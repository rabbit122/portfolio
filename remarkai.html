Bringing structure to a productthat was scaling faster than its design.

<!-- Overview -->
Company
RemarkAI
Role
Founding/Sole Designer
Duration
2.5 years
Platform
Web App · B2B SaaS

RemarkAI is a B2B AI video analytics platform — enterprise clients, multiple cameras, real-time incident detection. When I joined, the product was already running and signing clients. But it was built by engineers, for engineers.

No design system. No consistent interaction patterns. Interface language pulled straight from machine learning documentation. And for the first 1.5 years: no product manager.

<!-- What I actually did -->

I shipped a lot of features.
That was the baseline.

P0 features, client-driven, tight deadlines — sometimes three days from brief to delivery. That part came with the territory.

I also ran competitive reviews.

With no PM, I ran quarterly competitive reviews to make sure we weren't solving problems the market had already moved past.

<!-- The accumulating debt -->

But alongside the features,
I saw the debts — and fixed them.

Like most early-stage startups, RemarkAI prioritised shipping speed over consistency. By the time I joined, that debt was already accumulating.

The signs were already there — our ML engineers were being pulled into client onboarding calls because the interface was too complex to explain without them. I recognised this as a symptom of accumulated UX debt, and the most expensive kind: the kind you pay for with your best engineers' time.

I started systematically tackling it in three categories: the language the product used, the trust it was eroding, and the foundations that didn't exist yet.

Judgment
01

The product was speaking the wrong language.

If the user has to ask what this means, the design has already failed.

Users were confused. Engineers were constantly pulled into support calls. Everyone assumed users needed more training.

I thought the problem was somewhere else.

The interface was using machine learning terminology, Confidence, IoU threshold, Activity Level, Suppression. Those language that made complete sense to the team that built it, and almost no sense to the operations managers actually using it.

I couldn't run formal user research. So I used the sales team as a proxy — no technical background, but real operational goals. Close enough.

Before
ML terminology, fully exposed
Confidence 59% · IoU threshold · Suppression · Activity Level — all parameters visible at once, no hierarchy.
After
Plain language, progressive disclosure
Alert Reliability 59% with visual indicator. Secondary parameters moved behind a disclosure layer. Settings pages got visual explanations instead of raw labels.
✓

Shipped · Reduced engineer involvement in client onboarding calls

Judgment
02

Give users control. Don't just reduce noise.

False alerts were eroding client trust. Rain, neon lights, extreme camera angles — the AI would misfire, and users would get flooded with notifications.

The engineering solution was to adjust alert timing intervals. Reasonable. Practical. And it didn't solve the actual problem.

My read was different: users weren't frustrated by the frequency of alerts. They were frustrated by the lack of control. They knew their environment. The system didn't.

The problem
The solution
Exclusion Zones

Letting users draw areas directly on the camera feed and mark them as permanently excluded from detection. You know that neon sign triggers false fires every night? Tell the system to ignore it.

It took a few months to push through. The feature shipped. False alert complaints dropped.

✓

Exclusion Zones shipped · False alert complaints dropped

!

User feedback mechanism — design ready, not built · Blocked by engineering complexity

Judgment
03

Nobody asked for foundations. I built them anyway.

I tried to build design culture — workshops, sprint retrospectives. Most of it didn't survive contact with the reality of that environment.

So I focused on what I could actually control: the foundations. Design system, consistent interaction patterns, a shared visual language across the product.

Nobody asked for these. But without them, every new feature would have made the product harder to use than the one before it.

What I left behind

A product that non-engineers could understand. An interface that didn't need ML experts in every client call. A design system that gave future designers something to build on. And the judgment to know when to push back — on engineering shortcuts, on feature additions, and on the assumption that more complexity means more capability.
