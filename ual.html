UAL Graduate Showcase & Design SystemRedefining what a university showcase platform is for.

UAL is one of the world's top two art and design universities. Every year, its Graduate Showcase is the most important public moment for tens of thousands of students — the first time their work is seen by the world.

When the pandemic cancelled in-person events, UAL built an online showcase fast. I joined as a contractor in year two — the 2.0 version — alongside one other designer. Three months in, I moved onto the Design System team for the remaining contract period.

Choosing
Not to Play It Safe

Situation

When I joined, there was already a direction on the table. An external usability report had flagged high bounce rate and surface-level problems — filters hard to use, difficult to find a specific student's work. A colleague had produced layout-level wireframe revisions in response. The team reviewed it. It worked. Nobody could say it was wrong.

But nobody was quite convinced it was right either.

My judgment

Layout changes address what users struggle with. They don't address why users leave. The external report gave us symptoms; it didn't give us intent. If we shipped this direction, we'd have a tidier version of the same platform — but we still wouldn't know what students actually needed from it. So we needed to go back to users. Fast.

Why this was worth the time

Our office was directly above one of the UAL colleges. Testing didn't mean recruiting, scheduling, or a research budget. It meant walking downstairs to the canteen. The cost was low. The risk of not doing it — spending the rest of the contract optimising the wrong thing — was much higher.

What it immediately changed

It shifted the team's shared understanding of what the problem actually was. Before testing, we were solving for usability friction. After testing, we were solving for something more fundamental — which I'll come to next.

What we had
— External usability report (high bounce rate flagged)
— Layout-level wireframe revisions
— A direction that worked, but felt flat
What we chose to do first
— Guerrilla testing with real UAL students
— Open questions, not task prompts
— Cost: walking downstairs

The Insight That
Reframed Everything

Situation

The guerrilla testing surfaced something unexpected. Through open questions — What does this platform mean to you? What do you want it to do for you after graduation? — a pattern emerged immediately: students weren't thinking of this as an annual event. They were thinking of it as their first portfolio. Their first professional presence.

For UAL students — whether you're in fashion, UX, photography, or stage design — you need a portfolio to get work. Many don't have one yet when they graduate. The showcase was already doing this job accidentally. Nobody had designed for it intentionally.

My judgment

This wasn't a UX problem. It was a positioning problem. The platform had been designed as a ceremony. Students were trying to use it as career infrastructure. Those are two completely different design briefs.

Three Modes,
One Shift

Situation

Mission defined. Now: what does that actually mean in the product? We identified three things students wanted from this platform beyond showing work: sell their physical work, connect with employers, find collaborators.

My judgment

We didn't need to build a marketplace. We needed three flags at upload — Sell / Hire me / Collaborate — and a redesigned filter logic and hero section built around these three modes.

Why this over a full rebuild

The timeline was fixed and non-negotiable. UAL had assembled a contractor team — developers and designers — specifically to hit one hard deadline: the graduation ceremony. Missing it wasn't an option. So every decision had to answer: what delivers the most meaningful change within what we can actually ship?

Three decisions worth unpacking
Sell

Historically, the in-person showcase functioned like a creative market; people genuinely bought things. That layer had disappeared online. We reconnected it: if a student had a shop listing, 'Buy' linked directly there. If not, it defaulted to their Instagram, DM, or email based on their preference. No new payment infrastructure. Minimal engineering lift.

Employ

Students wanted to be actively findable to employers — not just passively visible. Framing this as an intentional signal rather than passive presence was the design shift.

Collaborate

This addressed a pattern we hadn't expected — fashion students need photographers, stage designers need people. Cross-discipline collaboration was already happening; we just needed to make it findable.

Result

The landing page shifted from "come see our works" to "find the world's most creative emerging talent", with three clear entry points. UAL graduates around 3,000 students a year. At peak, accounting for graduates' immediate networks, the showcase conservatively reaches an estimated 15,000 users in a concentrated window around graduation. The platform now had a reason to keep them there.
