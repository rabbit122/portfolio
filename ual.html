The brief was to fix bounce rate. We ended up changing what the platform was for.

UAL is one of the world's top two art and design universities. Every year, its Graduate Showcase is the most important public moment for tens of thousands of students — the first time their work is seen by the world.

When the pandemic cancelled in-person events, UAL built an online showcase fast. I joined as a contractor in year two — the 2.0 version — alongside one other designer. Three months in, I moved onto the Design System team for the remaining contract period.

Choosing
Not to Play It Safe

When I joined, there was already a direction on the table. An external usability report had flagged a 63% bounce rate and identified friction problems: unclear starting point, weak filtering, hard to find specific students' work. A colleague had produced layout-level wireframe revisions in response.

The team reviewed the wireframes. They worked. Nobody could say they were wrong. But nobody was convinced they were right either. We had moved things around, but we hadn't moved anything forward.

A critical observation about the external report: The report told us where user got stuck. It didn't tell us what students needed the platform to do for them. Those are two different questions.

Early brainstorming — sketches
My judgment

Layout changes address where users struggle. They don't address why users leave. If we shipped this direction, we'd have a tidier version of the same platform, this is the safer move. But we still wouldn't know what students actually needed from it.

Instead, I proposed a round of guerrilla testing before going further.

Why this was worth the time

Our office was directly above one of the UAL colleges. Testing didn't mean recruiting or a research budget, it meant walking downstairs to the canteen. The cost was 3 days. The risk of not doing it was spending the rest of the contract optimising the wrong thing.

The Insight That
Reframed Everything

Guerrilla testing. Open questions, not task prompts.

"What do you want this platform to do for you after graduation?"
"What does being on this showcase mean to you?"

A pattern emerged immediately, it wasn't about navigation or filters:

Students were thinking of it as their first portfolio. Their first professional presence. For UAL students, whether they're in fashion, ceramics, UX or stage design. They need a portfolio to get work. Many don't have one yet when they graduate. The showcase was already doing this job accidentally. Nobody had designed for it intentionally.

Four Modes,
One Shift

Mission defined. Now: what does that actually mean in the product?

We identified four things different visitors needed from this platform, and they were fundamentally different from each other:

My judgment

We needed to redesign the information architecture around user intent — not institutional structure.

The old platform was organised around institutional structure — colleges, subjects, courses. It assumed you already knew where to look. The new platform was organised around intent — what you came here to do. That's a different starting point entirely, and it changed every design decision downstream.

Students signal. Visitors discover.

When students uploaded their work, they could flag their intent — available for hire, open to collaboration, selling physical work, or simply showcasing. These flags became the foundation of the new discovery system.

On the visitor side, we translated these four student intents into four entry points on the homepage. The mechanism was simple: clicking any entry point filtered the entire showcase to show only the relevant students.

If you're an employer, you click Employ — the page refreshes to show only students actively looking for work. Passive visibility becomes active signalling. The same logic applies across all four modes.

Why not a full rebuild

The deadline was fixed and non-negotiable. UAL had assembled a contractor team, developers and designers, specifically to hit one hard deadline: the graduation ceremony. Every decision had to answer: what delivers the most meaningful change within what we can actually ship?

The landing page shifted from "Discover the next generation of creatives" to "Employ or Collaborate with the creators & innovators of tomorrow, Explore the work & Buy original pieces".

Validating
the Direction

Final design ready. One question remained: does this actually work?

The team and I ran a second round of guerrilla testing, this time testing the new design. Same approach: in context, no formal recruitment. UAL had a physical shop selling student work right below our office, which gave us direct access to buyers. For employers, we reached out through our own networks.

Nobody asked for this. We did it because we wanted to leave something useful behind — not just shipped files, but validated direction and a set of user insights that whoever joined in 2023 could actually build on.

What we produced

Customer Journey Maps across Graduate Students, Explorers, Collaborators, and Employers, with painpoints and opportunities mapped for each.

We also ran a retrospective session to reflect on the process and define handover standards for what came next.

The brief was to reduce bounce rate. We ended up redefining what the platform was for.

Not because the original brief was wrong — bounce rate was a real problem. But bounce rate is a symptom. The cause was that the platform had been designed for one audience (visitors to an annual event) while being used by another (students building their first professional presence).

Once we understood that, every decision became clearer. The filter logic, the hero copy, the four entry points, the buy flow — none of these were arbitrary design choices. They were answers to a question the previous version hadn't asked.

"Identify the right problem before touching the design."
