Context

After the Graduate Showcase project wrapped, I moved onto UAL's Design System team for the remaining two months of my contract.

UAL's design system covered eight colleges and multiple digital products, main website, Graduate Showcase, Arts Temps. It was integrated with the playbook and broadly followed across products. By most standards, it was in good shape.

The brief was straightforward: audit the existing system, identify gaps, recommend updates.

A small team of three: a Design Lead and two contractors, myself included.

<!-- Impact 01 -->

The Audit

What we did

We screenshotted every page across UAL's product suite — homepage, college pages, course pages and more. Comparing each against the existing system.

For every element we found, we asked two questions: does this follow the system, or has it drifted? And if it's new, should it be added?

I was responsible for auditing specific components across all page types and breakpoints — including Promote Card, Notification, Footer, and Header. Checking for inconsistencies in sizing, spacing, and layout behaviour throughout the product suite.

What we found

The system itself was largely sound. Most pages followed the spec. Deviations were either minor frontend inconsistencies or intentional additions that hadn't yet been documented.

Two components needed to be added as genuine new additions: a new Promote Card size introduced in the Graduate Showcase, and the updated Filter structure — both outputs of the website project I'd just completed. The work I'd done on the Showcase fed directly back into the system.

<!-- Impact 02 -->

Finding the Real Problem

I moved on to the next part of the brief: organising, adding to, and documenting what we'd found.

I started with Promote Card. I laid out the component, variants, states. It looked complete. But working through it, something felt off.

What I'd produced was a display. Here's the component. Here's what it looks like. But there was nothing to tell a designer when to use it, how to use it correctly, or what accessibility considerations applied. If someone new joined the team tomorrow and opened this file, they'd see the component, but they wouldn't know what to do with it.

My judgment

The current documentation had been built to show what existed. It hadn't been built to help people use it.

I looked at how mature design systems document themselves. Atlassian, Microsoft and a few others. The pattern that works consistently: Every component has at least four layers. Overview, Usage, Accessibility, and Development status, linking the design directly to its implementation in Storybook. Not just 'here it is', but what it's for, how to use it, what to watch out for, and where it stands in the codebase.

I wrote a detailed proposal for updated the entire documentation using this framework and presented it to team. Everyone agrees with this approach.

Why this matters

When work through it, I kept thinking about how I'd actually use this system day-to-day. That's when I noticed the system itself was hard to reference. A component library that's slow and clunky to navigate just doesn't get used as much as it should.

New documentation Structure
Before
Component name
└── [variants displayed, no explanation]
After
Component name
├── Overview — what this component is for
├── Usage — how and when to use it
├── Accessibility — what to watch out for
└── Development status — implementation state in Storybook

What Got Built

My contract ended before the restructure was complete, but I followed up later to see how it landed. The structure had been implemented in full, exactly as proposed.

What I delivered: the audit, two new components, and a documentation framework that the team implemented after I left.

The components were expected. The framework wasn't asked for. But it was the part that made the system usable for the next person who opened it.

The connection back to the Showcase

The two new components added during this project, the Promote Card size and the Filter structure. Both came directly from the Graduate Showcase work. Design decisions made in one project fed back into the shared system that served all of UAL's digital products. That's what a design system is supposed to do.
